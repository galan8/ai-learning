# Overreliance

**2023: LLM09 Overreliance. 2025: LLM09 Misinformation**, renamed and refocused. The 2023 framing is about humans over trusting output; the 2025 framing is about the model generating and propagating falsehood, with overreliance as the contributing factor that turns it into harm.

Both halves matter: **the model produces something false, and a human or a system acts on it without verification.**

## 1. What it is

Relying on model output without verifying its accuracy or authenticity. Models fabricate information, contradict themselves, invent citations and hyperlinks, and do all of it **fluently and confidently**, which is precisely what makes it dangerous.

## 2. Why hallucination is structural, not a bug

From the Chapter 2 fundamentals: an LLM predicts the next statistically likely token. It has no representation of truth, no mechanism for knowing what it does not know, and no confidence signal exposed to the user by default.

**A model knows only what it knows, and when it does not know, it produces the statistically plausible answer rather than no answer.** Where its training data is thin, it bridges the gap by generating something that looks like what an answer would look like. Fluency is constant regardless of accuracy, so the output gives the reader no signal to distinguish the two.

This is why hallucination cannot be eliminated by better prompting. It is a property of the mechanism.

## 3. Where it does damage

1. **Sensitive domains.** Medical, legal, financial and safety contexts, where a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer.
2. **Bias propagation.** Biased responses inherited from training data and presented as neutral.
3. **Automated pipelines.** Where output feeds another system with no human reading it at all.
4. **Software supply chain.** Covered below, because it converts a hallucination into a code execution vector.

## 4. Package hallucination and slopsquatting

The most security relevant form of hallucination, because it turns a wrong answer into an attack.

Models recommending code frequently invent package names that do not exist. If an attacker registers the invented name, every developer who follows that recommendation installs the attacker's package.

**The demonstration.** Bar Lanyado of Lasso Security observed models repeatedly hallucinating a Python package called `huggingface-cli` (the real installation is different). He registered an **empty** package under that name on PyPI as a benign proof of concept. It received in the region of **15,000 to 30,000 downloads** over about three months, depending on the source reporting it, and **Alibaba's GraphTranslator repository recommended the hallucinated package in its README**, showing real uptake by real projects. Lanyado's testing found hallucination rates around 20% across several models.

**The academic measurement.** "We Have a Package for You!" (USENIX Security 2025, Spracklen et al.) tested **576,000 code samples from 16 LLMs** across Python and JavaScript. **19.7% of recommended packages were hallucinated**, producing **205,474 unique fabricated names**. Commercial models hallucinated at around 5.2%, open source models at around 21.7%.

**The property that makes it exploitable:** hallucinations are **repeatable**. 43% of hallucinated names recurred across all ten re-runs of the same prompt, and 58% appeared more than once. An attacker does not need to guess; they can enumerate what a model reliably invents and register those names. Frontier models have since improved to roughly 5%, but a shared set of names is invented identically across all tested models, so the threat persists.

**Slopsquatting**, the term for registering hallucinated package names, was coined by **Seth Larson of the Python Software Foundation in April 2025**. It is typosquatting where the LLM makes the typo.

## 5. Overreliance with legal consequences

**Mata v. Avianca** (S.D.N.Y., June 2023). Attorneys submitted a brief citing **six nonexistent cases** generated by ChatGPT. Judge Castel sanctioned the two lawyers **$5,000** and ordered them to notify the judges named in the fabricated opinions. The problem has since scaled into the hundreds of documented instances worldwide, tracked in public databases of AI hallucinated citations in court filings.

**Moffatt v. Air Canada** (BCCRT, February 2024). Air Canada's chatbot invented a bereavement fare policy that did not exist. The tribunal found negligent misrepresentation, awarded CAN$812.02, and rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own actions.

Together these establish both directions of liability: **professionals are liable for unverified AI output they rely on, and organisations are liable for false statements their AI makes.**

## 6. Mitigations

**From the lesson:**

1. **Provide domain knowledge through fine tuning**, so the model has less need to bridge gaps.
2. **Equip the model with RAG** for current, grounded information.
3. **Ensure training data is accurate and untainted.**
4. **Break complex prompts into smaller step by step prompts**, which reduces the room for fabrication in any single step.

**Additions:**

5. **Cite sources and show them.** Ground answers in retrieved documents and display which ones, so a user can verify rather than trust.
6. **Expose confidence and refuse when low.** A system that can say it does not know is far safer than one that always answers.
7. **Verify machine checkable claims automatically.** For package recommendations, check the registry before presenting the name. For citations, resolve them. This converts a human verification burden into a deterministic check.
8. **Keep a human in the loop proportional to consequence**, and be explicit with users about what the system can get wrong.
9. **Note the limit of RAG.** Grounding reduces hallucination on questions the documents answer. It does nothing for questions they do not, where the model falls back on its own knowledge, and users trust RAG systems *more* because they cite sources. That combination makes ungrounded answers in a RAG system more dangerous, not less.

## 7. Summary

1. Renamed to Misinformation in 2025; the model generates falsehood and a human or system acts on it unverified.
2. Hallucination is structural: the model predicts plausible tokens and has no notion of truth or of its own ignorance.
3. Fluency is constant regardless of accuracy, so the output carries no signal of reliability.
4. Package hallucination affects roughly 20% of recommendations and is **repeatable**, which makes slopsquatting practical.
5. Courts have established liability in both directions: for relying on AI output and for what your AI says.
6. Defence is grounding, citation, automated verification of checkable claims, and calibrated human review.
